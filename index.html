Portfolio clustering: four product archetypes

The 50,535 usable product rows across 39 processed catalogs collapse into 14,166 unique products (brand + normalized name). Each product is described by five features aggregated over every catalog appearance — median price, promo exposure, discount depth, catalog persistence, and price volatility — then standardized and clustered with scikit-learn KMeans. Silhouette analysis picks k = 4, and the four clusters turn out to be four very different jobs a product can do in a catalog.

C1 · 45.7% of products

One-cycle full-pricers

Appear in a single catalog, at full price (98% never promoted), then rotate out. The novelty engine: makeup shades and seasonal variety from Una, Make B. and Quem Disse, Berenice?

R$63 median · 1 appearance · 2% promo

C3 · 28.2% of products

Born on promotion

Also single-appearance — but 94% arrive already discounted. The priciest archetype (R$78 median) and where gift kits (“Presente…”) concentrate: offers built as offers.

R$78 median · 1 appearance · 94% promo · −21%

C2 · 21.8% of products

The evergreen backbone

The recurring assortment: median 9 catalog appearances with rock-stable prices (volatility 1.5%) and a promotion roughly every fourth appearance. This is the shelf the rotation revolves around.

R$65 median · 9 appearances · 27% promo · −21%

C0 · 4.3% of products

The managed few

The only archetype whose prices actually move (volatility 17%): persistent staples that are repriced and heavily promoted. Active price management is rare — and 65% of it is Natura.

R$59 median · 6 appearances · 38% promo · −24%

The whole architecture in one picture: two dense single-appearance columns on the left — full-price novelty (green, bottom) and promo-born kits (blue, top) — with the persistent backbone (amber) and the actively managed set (violet) stretching to the right.

What the clusters reveal

  • The catalog is a showcase, not a shelf. 74% of unique products appear in exactly one cycle; only about a quarter of the portfolio is recurring assortment.
  • Promotion is a birth trait, not a lifecycle event. Single-appearance products either never promote (C1) or debut on promotion (C3) — there is almost no middle ground.
  • The house discount is ~20%. Wherever promotion happens, the median discount lands at 21–24% — a standardized markdown, not product-level price tuning.
  • True price management is the exception. Just 4% of products combine repricing with heavy promotion — and that set skews strongly Natura (65/35).
  • Both brands run the same playbook. Aside from the managed set, every archetype splits roughly 50/50 between Natura and O Boticário.
Method & limitations

Features per unique product: log median regular price, share of appearances on promotion, median discount when promoted, log catalog appearances, and price coefficient of variation. Standardized, clustered with KMeans (k = 3–7 tested; silhouette peaks at k = 4, score 0.43). PCA is used for the 2-D projection only.

  • Products are matched by normalized name within brand; renamed or re-bundled items count as separate products, which inflates the single-appearance clusters somewhat.
  • Catalog coverage differs by brand and year (39 processed catalogs, 2025–26); persistence is measured against the catalogs available, not the brands’ full calendars.
  • Promotional flags come from vision-extracted catalog pages; page-level extraction errors propagate into promo rates.
